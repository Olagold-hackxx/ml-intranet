Theoretical Machine Learning

A 6-Month Chapter-by-Chapter Study Plan

Built from Shai Ben-David's 'Understanding Machine Learning' (UWaterloo 2015) and Stanford CS229M.

Page 2 of 2 — companion to the Applied ML plan.

How this plan differs from the applied track

The applied track teaches you how to build ML systems. This track teaches you why they work, when they are guaranteed to work, and what their fundamental limitations are. It is shorter in weeks (≈24) but denser per week — expect to spend as much time on one proof as you would on a whole applied lecture.

You will only need three things:

-   A textbook. The companion book for Ben-David's course is 'Understanding Machine Learning: From Theory to Algorithms' by Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David. This is the single most important resource in this plan — the course IS the book.

-   Paper and pen. Theoretical ML is done on paper, not on a keyboard. Budget at least 60% of your time to working through proofs by hand.

-   Mathematical maturity. Real analysis, linear algebra, probability (measure-theoretic is a plus but not required). If any of these is shaky, fix it first; the rest of the plan assumes them.

Prerequisites: honest self-check

Before starting Week 1, you should be able to, without looking anything up:

-   State and prove Markov's inequality, Chebyshev's inequality, and Hoeffding's inequality.

-   State the weak and strong laws of large numbers and sketch their proofs.

-   Compute expectations and variances for standard distributions (Bernoulli, Binomial, Gaussian).

-   Diagonalize a symmetric matrix; state the spectral theorem.

-   Work comfortably with epsilon-delta definitions, suprema/infima, and convergence of sequences.

If any of these is not solid, spend 2–4 weeks on Ross's 'A First Course in Probability' and Axler's 'Linear Algebra Done Right' before starting this plan. The time saved later is enormous.

Plan overview

The plan is split into two passes:

-   Pass 1 (Weeks 1–12) — Classical statistical learning theory with Ben-David's 'Understanding Machine Learning' course. This is the foundation. You learn PAC learning, VC dimension, uniform convergence, boosting, SVMs, convex learning, and regularization — all with proofs.

-   Pass 2 (Weeks 13–24) — Modern learning theory with Stanford CS229M. This builds on Pass 1 to cover concentration in high dimensions, uniform convergence refinements, online learning, and the theoretical frontier around overparameterized models.

Each week's row tells you exactly which lectures, which chapter(s) of Shalev-Shwartz & Ben-David (SSBD), which theorems to internalize, and which end-of-chapter exercises to complete. The 'exercises' column is non-negotiable — you do not understand a theorem until you have used it in a problem.

Pass 2 deliverables

-   Concentration inequality cheat sheet — every inequality with its proof sketch on a single page.

-   A proof portfolio: the full proofs of the 5 most important theorems in Pass 2 (your choice, but must include the Fundamental Theorem restatement, the Rademacher generalization bound, the implicit-bias result for logistic regression, an NTK derivation, and an online learning regret bound).

-   A capstone paper critique — 4 pages, publication-style writing.

How to actually study this material

The 4-pass method per lecture

-   Pass 1 — Watch the lecture at 1x speed. Take rough notes. Do not pause to understand proofs fully; just follow.

-   Pass 2 — Read the corresponding textbook chapter. Stop at every theorem. Write the statement in your own words. Skip to Pass 3 before trying to understand the proof.

-   Pass 3 — Close the book. Re-derive the key results on paper. Struggle. Peek only when fully stuck, and only at the next line.

-   Pass 4 — Do 2–3 exercises. If you can't do an exercise, the theorem isn't yet yours. Go back to Pass 3.

What not to do

-   Do not take LaTeX notes during lectures. The goal is thinking, not transcription. Handwrite, and rewrite into LaTeX only if a result matters to you later.

-   Do not skip exercises because 'you understand the proof'. Exercises are where understanding is tested — the rest is illusion.

-   Do not try to code everything. This is theory. A small amount of simulation (e.g. plotting generalization gap vs sample size) is useful as a sanity check, but time spent coding is time not spent proving.

-   Do not skim the non-uniform-learnability or SRM chapters because they feel abstract. They are the connective tissue of the whole theory.

Pacing — realistic expectations

10–15 hours per week is roughly right. If you're doing less than 8, you will forget material faster than you learn it and should slow down rather than stretch the plan thin. If you're doing more than 20, you're probably reading passively rather than proving — cut back and do more exercises.

Books to read alongside (optional but excellent)

-   Mohri, Rostamizadeh & Talwalkar — 'Foundations of Machine Learning'. A harder, more modern alternative to SSBD. Use it as a second opinion when SSBD is unclear.

-   Vershynin — 'High-Dimensional Probability'. Indispensable for Pass 2 Weeks 13–16.

-   Wainwright — 'High-Dimensional Statistics'. For going deeper after Pass 2 ends.

-   Bach — 'Learning Theory from First Principles'. A recent, unified, very clean treatment. Good as a capstone re-read.

Final self-test

At Week 24 you should be able to, on a blank sheet of paper and with no references:

-   State and prove the Fundamental Theorem of Statistical Learning.

-   Derive a generalization bound for linear classifiers using Rademacher complexity.

-   Explain, with a proof sketch, why gradient descent on logistic regression implicitly regularizes toward the max-margin solution.

-   Read a new COLT paper and identify which of the tools above it relies on.

If you can do all four, you have a genuine foundation in theoretical ML — enough to read the modern research literature unaided, and enough to decide whether to go deeper (measure concentration, optimal transport, information theory) or back to applied work with sharper eyes.

Using both plans together

The two plans are designed to run in parallel if you have the time (≈20 hours/week combined), or sequentially if you'd rather focus. My recommendation: start with the applied plan's Phase 1 (8 weeks), then begin Pass 1 of the theoretical plan while continuing Phase 2 of the applied plan. The two reinforce each other — every time you train a model in the applied track, you will have sharper questions about why it works, which the theoretical track helps you answer.

Do not try to master both tracks perfectly in parallel from Week 1 — you will burn out. Pick one as primary and the other as secondary, and swap them at the phase boundaries.
