A 12-Month Chapter-by-Chapter Study Plan

Built from Stanford CS229, CS221, CS230, CS231N, CS224N, CS224U, CS236, CS234, CS329H, CS336, CME295, and related lectures.

How to use this plan

This plan sequences 13 Stanford-level courses into a single 12-month curriculum (≈10–12 hours/week). It is chapter-by-chapter: each row tells you which specific lectures to watch that week and what to produce by the end of the week. You do not watch every course front-to-back in order — that would mean 300+ hours of overlapping foundations. Instead, the plan interleaves courses by topic so that what you learn one week feeds directly into the next.

Structure:

-   Phase 1 — Foundations (Weeks 1–8): classical ML, math, and the learning framework.

-   Phase 2 — Deep Learning Core (Weeks 9–16): neural nets, CNNs, training at scale.

-   Phase 3 — Sequence Models & NLP (Weeks 17–26): from RNNs to transformers to LLMs.

-   Phase 4 — Generative Models & RL (Weeks 27–36): VAEs, diffusion, GANs, RL, RLHF.

-   Phase 5 — Frontier & Systems (Weeks 37–48): LLMs from scratch, evaluation, scaling, capstone.

Weekly routine (recommended):

-   2–3 lectures (≈3–4 hours)

-   Course problem set or paper reading (≈3 hours)

-   One hands-on implementation in PyTorch or JAX (≈3–4 hours)

-   Short written summary — one page in your own words, per week (≈1 hour)

Rule of thumb: if a course's first 2–3 lectures cover material you already know cold from another course, skip to where it diverges. The table tells you where courses overlap.

Goal: build the mathematical and conceptual spine for everything else. By the end of Phase 1 you should be able to derive linear & logistic regression from scratch, explain bias-variance and regularization clearly, and implement a small neural net without a framework.

Phase 1 deliverables

-   A small GitHub repo titled foundations-ml with NumPy implementations of: linear regression, logistic regression, SVM (linear & kernel), MLP, k-means, PCA.

-   An 8-page note (one page per week) summarizing key derivations and intuitions in your own words.

-   One mini-project: Titanic or a comparable Kaggle dataset solved with three different classical models and a short write-up comparing them.

Capstone (Weeks 45–48)

Pick one of the three tracks below and spend four full weeks on it. The deliverable is a public write-up plus code.

-   Track A — Training track: Train a domain-specific small LM (e.g. legal, medical, or code) end-to-end: data collection, tokenizer, pretraining, SFT, evaluation.

-   Track B — Research-reproduction track: Reproduce a recent paper of your choice (e.g. DPO, Mamba, Mixture-of-Experts). Write a detailed reproduction report with ablations.

-   Track C — Applied-systems track: Build a production-grade agent or RAG system with retrieval, evaluation harness, and monitoring. Treat it like a real product.

Final deliverables

-   A portfolio repository containing every week's work, with a top-level README that tells the story of the year.

-   A capstone write-up (5–10 pages) posted publicly.

-   A list of 5 open questions the year raised for you — these become your research direction.

Practical notes on pacing, skipping, and reading alongside

When to skip lectures

Several of these courses overlap heavily — especially the first 3–4 lectures of CS229, CS230, CS224N, and CS231N, which all re-introduce gradient descent and neural networks. Once you have done it once (in CS229), skip those lectures in later courses and jump to where the course specializes. The plan above already does this interleaving, but use your judgment if a lecture feels fully redundant.

Books to read alongside

-   During Phase 1: 'Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning' (Bishop) or 'The Elements of Statistical Learning' (Hastie, Tibshirani, Friedman). Pick one, don't switch.

-   During Phase 2–3: 'Deep Learning' (Goodfellow, Bengio, Courville) for the theory chapters, and Sebastian Raschka's 'Build a Large Language Model (From Scratch)' as a practical companion to CS336.

-   During Phase 4: 'Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction' (Sutton & Barto), second edition — the canonical RL text.

Papers to read — minimum viable set

-   Phase 2: AlexNet, VGG, ResNet, BatchNorm, Dropout, Adam.

-   Phase 3: Word2Vec, Attention Is All You Need, BERT, GPT-2, T5, RoPE, FlashAttention.

-   Phase 4: VAE (Kingma & Welling), GAN (Goodfellow), DDPM (Ho et al.), DQN (Mnih), PPO (Schulman), InstructGPT, DPO.

-   Phase 5: Chinchilla, LLaMA, Mixtral, the original RLHF paper (Christiano et al. 2017).

Self-assessment — what it should feel like at the end

If the plan has worked, by Week 48 you should be able to do three things without looking anything up: (1) write down the loss, architecture, and training procedure for any of VAE, GAN, diffusion, transformer LM, PPO, DPO; (2) look at a deep-learning training curve and give a short list of plausible bugs and fixes; (3) read a new paper on arXiv and place it accurately in the taxonomy of the field. If any of those three feels shaky, go back and identify the week where the gap started.
